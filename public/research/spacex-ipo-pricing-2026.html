Research Note · No. 3

The Price Was Right

SpaceX sold shares at $135. They closed the first day at $161, hit $226 four days later, then fell to $105. People say the bankers left $16.6 billion on the table. They did not.

Zhiye's Market Notes · September 5, 2026 · Data through September 4

Verdict

The biggest share sale in history was priced almost exactly right. The first three days were the part that was wrong.

SpaceX sold 638.9 million shares at $135 and raised $85.7 billion, the largest IPO ever. The shares closed the first day at $160.95 — a jump of 19.2%. Across 9,343 American IPOs since 1980, the average first-day jump is 19.0%. On the largest deal ever attempted, with no other company to compare it to, the bankers landed within a fifth of a percentage point of the 45-year average.

The famous "$16.6 billion left on the table" is real arithmetic but a weak fact. It was measured on a day when only about 5% of the company could be traded. A price set by 5% of the shares does not tell you what the other 95% was worth.

We got to test that. On August 6 the number of tradable shares roughly doubled. If the June price had only been high because shares were scarce, that was the day it should have broken. Instead the stock rose 6%, and it now trades at $149.74 — 11% above the price the bankers picked.

1. What "priced wrong" has to mean

When a company sells shares to the public for the first time, someone has to guess a price. Guess too low and the company hands free money to whoever bought at that price. Guess too high and the shares sink and the buyers are angry.

The usual way people score this is the first-day jump: the gap between the price the company sold at and the price the shares closed at that evening. The gap, times the number of shares, is called money left on the table — the extra cash the company could have raised if it had picked the higher price.

The claim to test. SpaceX's shares jumped 19.2% on day one. So the bankers set the price far too low, and cost the company billions it should have collected.

That sounds damning. It is also the single most common criticism made of every large IPO, which is a reason to check it rather than repeat it.

2. Test one — was a 19% jump unusual?

No. It is the average, almost to the decimal.

Research covering 9,343 US IPOs from 1980 to 2025 puts the mean first-day return at 19.0%. SpaceX's was 19.2%. Over those 45 years companies raised $1.2 trillion and left about $250 billion on the table — 20.8% of what they collected. SpaceX left $16.6 billion derived on $85.7 billion, which is 19.3%.

So the first test does not find a scandal. It finds a completely ordinary result, achieved on a deal seven times larger than any before it.

3. Test two — the number was measured on 5% of the company

Here is the part the headline misses. On the day the shares jumped 19%, only about 5% of SpaceX was available to trade. Everything else was locked up: insiders for 366 days, other early backers on a staggered 180-day schedule.

That matters because price is set by supply and demand. If a huge number of people want shares and almost none exist, the price goes up — and that tells you nothing about what buyers would have paid for twenty times as many shares. "Money left on the table" quietly assumes SpaceX could have sold every share at $160.95. It could not have. Nobody offered to buy 638 million shares at that price; a small pool of buyers bid up a small pool of shares.

The number is also strangely fragile. Measure it on a different day and it changes sign.

4. Test three — the day the shares stopped being scarce

If the June price was only high because shares were rare, there is an obvious way to find out: wait until they stop being rare.

That happened on August 6, when about 911.5 million shares came unlocked — roughly doubling the number that could be traded. The expectation was ugly. Doubling the supply of anything usually drops its price, and the stock had already fallen from $225.64 to a low of $104.83 in the weeks before.

It did not break. The stock rose about 6% on the day the shares were unlocked, and has climbed roughly 19% in the four weeks since. It now trades at $149.74 — above the $135 offer, and the highest since July. The extra supply arrived and the market absorbed it.

That is the strongest evidence in this note. A price propped up only by scarcity does not survive the end of scarcity. This one did.

5. What this does not excuse

Two honest points against the verdict.

Anyone who bought the excitement lost money. Buy at the $225.64 peak and you are down 34%. For about seven weeks the stock traded below $135, meaning the people who bought in the IPO were underwater too. "Priced right" describes the bankers' job, not every buyer's outcome.

And $16.6 billion is still a real transfer. Being normal is not the same as being free. Roughly a fifth of what SpaceX raised went to whoever was allocated shares at $135 rather than to the company. That is how the system works, and it is worth noticing that "how the system works" costs sellers about 20% every time.
