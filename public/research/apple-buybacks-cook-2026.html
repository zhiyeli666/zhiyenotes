What $877 Billion Bought

Tim Cook spent $877 billion buying back Apple's own shares — more than almost any company on earth is worth. People say that is what lifted the stock. It bought less than a fifth of it.

Zhiye's Market Notes · September 12, 2026 · Data through September 11

Verdict

False. Buybacks were the smallest of the three things that lifted Apple — and the one that cost the most.

Tim Cook ran Apple from August 2011 to September 1 this year. Over those fifteen years the share price rose about 2,230%, or roughly 24 times. That gain splits three ways, and buybacks come last:

Profit growth did about half of it (51%). Apple earned $25.9 billion in 2011 and around $130 billion now — five times as much. Investors paying more for each dollar of profit did about a third (31%). The price-to-earnings ratio went from 13.4 to 36.1. Buybacks did about a fifth (18%). The share count fell from roughly 26 billion to 14.6 billion, so each surviving share owns about 78% more of the company. derived

The striking part is the middle number. Investors simply changing their minds about what Apple is worth — which cost nobody anything — moved the stock almost twice as much as $877 billion of real cash did.

1. The claim, and the one formula that settles it

"Apple's rise was really just buybacks" is said constantly, and it is the kind of claim that sounds too clever to check. It is actually one of the easiest claims in investing to check, because a share price is made of exactly three things.

Share price = (profit ÷ number of shares) × the multiple people will pay

So a share price can only rise for three reasons: the company earns more, the shares are divided among fewer people, or people decide to pay more for each dollar of profit. Nothing else. Measure all three and there is no room left for opinion.

Buybacks act on the middle one only. A company buys its own shares and cancels them, so the same profit is split fewer ways. It is real — but it is one of three levers, and this note measures all three.

2. The three levers, multiplied

Because these three things multiply rather than add, the check is simple: multiply them and see whether you land on the real answer.

That last point matters. If some fourth force had secretly driven Apple's stock, these three numbers would not multiply to the right answer. They do. So the whole rise is accounted for, and now it only remains to say how the credit divides.

3. Splitting the credit

So the claim is false, and not narrowly. Buybacks are third of three. They contributed roughly 18% of a rise that profit growth contributed 51% of. If Apple had bought back nothing at all and everything else had happened, the stock would still have risen about twenty times instead of twenty-four.

4. The part nobody expects

The real surprise is not that buybacks came last. It is what came second.

In 2011 investors paid $13.40 for each dollar of Apple's yearly profit. Today they pay $36.10. Nothing about that change is a thing Apple did in the way that building an iPhone or repurchasing a share is a thing you do. It is a change of mind — the market deciding that Apple's profits are safer, more repeatable and more deserving of a premium than it believed in 2011, back when Apple was seen as a hit-driven gadget maker that might cool off.

That change of mind was worth 31% of the gain. The $877 billion was worth 18%. Cook's most valuable achievement may have been convincing the market what kind of company Apple is — and that shows up in the multiple, not in the cash flow statement.
