Research Note · No. 11

Made in Korea

On Friday morning South Korea's stock market fell 8% within minutes and trading was stopped. The shock came from Wall Street. The size of the fall came from Korea: a market that has concentrated into two companies, and whose shares have moved into the hands of people who are forced to sell when prices drop.

Zhiye's Notes · November 6, 2026 · Prices through the November 6 close

A market that stopped behaving like one

At 9:03 on Friday morning, the Korea Exchange stopped all trading for 20 minutes. The KOSPI, Korea's main index, had dropped 8% and fallen below 5,000 points, with Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix each down around 10%. It closed down 6.0% at 5,060, about 44% below its record in June. The night before, the Nasdaq had lost 5.5% in a sell-off of AI stocks.

For most markets, that would be the story of the year. For the KOSPI, it was an ordinary bad day.

The index more than doubled in under six months, then lost 44% in four and a half. It has fallen 5% or more on 23 days this year, against four days in the previous six years combined. It set a record for its worst day ever, down 12.1% on March 4, and a record for its best, up 17.9% on July 31, when SK Hynix rose by the 30% daily limit. Its swings have been more than four times the size of the S&P 500's derived.

The natural place to look for a cause is America. Korea has certainly become more sensitive to it. From 2023 to 2025, a 1% move in the Nasdaq was typically followed by a move of about 0.4% in the KOSPI the next morning. This year the figure is about 1.1% to 1.3% derived. But that link accounts for surprisingly little of what has happened. Since late May, the Nasdaq's overnight move explains only about 12% of the KOSPI's daily swings, and American chip stocks, the closest match, only about 16% derived.

Eight of the year's 15 worst days came after a calm night in New York. On July 13 the KOSPI fell 8.9% after the Nasdaq rose 0.3%. On July 28 it fell 10.8% after the Nasdaq slipped 0.2%. Friday's fall followed the worst night on the chart, and it was smaller than eleven of the others. Outside shocks have mattered, from a bad night for US chip stocks to the strikes on Iran that came before the record fall in March. But the same shocks reach every market, and very few fall 10% on them. To understand the size of these moves, you have to look at how the Korean market is built.

How a stock market became two stocks

A stock index is meant to spread risk across many companies. This year the memory-chip boom sent Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix up so far that the KOSPI stopped doing that. At the end of 2025 the two made up 34% of the market's value. By late June they made up 57%. Even after the crash they are 46%.

Separated like this, the KOSPI turns out to be two very different markets. The two chip stocks more than tripled by June and are still worth 64% more than at the start of the year. Everything else — banks, carmakers, shipbuilders — is down about 3% derived. The headline figure says Korean stocks are up 20% in 2026, but outside those two companies the market has gone nowhere.

This changes what the index measures. About two-thirds of the ₩3,300 trillion lost since June came from these two names derived. When they fall 13% or 14% in a day, as they did on July 28, the whole index has to fall about 10%, and there is no longer enough of anything else to cushion it. Almost half of Korea's stock market now depends on the price of a single product, so the index moves with the memory cycle.

The handover

Concentration explains why the index follows two stocks. It does not explain why those stocks swing so far. Companies this large normally move slowly, because they are held by big funds that are in no hurry. What changed this year is who owns them.

Foreign investors have been sellers through the whole rally, a net ₩217 trillion of Korean shares so far this year. By mid-October, about ₩173 trillion of what they had sold was Samsung and SK Hynix. Every share sold has a buyer, and a growing share of the buying came from Korean retail investors using borrowed money. Margin debt, the money investors borrow from brokers to buy shares, went from ₩27 trillion at the start of the year to a record ₩38.6 trillion in June.

This is the most important change in the market, and it does not appear in any price chart. A global pension fund that owns Samsung can sit through a 20% fall. An investor who bought Samsung with a loan may not get the choice: if the price drops far enough, the broker sells the shares to protect its money. As the rally went on, shares moved from owners who could wait to owners who could not. The higher the price went, the more fragile the market underneath it became.

Funds that have to sell

The second form of leverage is newer, and in some ways stranger. A Hong Kong fund offering twice the daily move of SK Hynix grew to $13 billion by June, briefly the largest of its kind in the world. On May 27, Korea launched 16 single-stock products of its own, mostly on the same idea, and retail investors put in a net ₩14 trillion. Goldman Sachs estimated that products listed abroad make up about half of Korea's total leveraged exposure.

These funds work in a way that matters a great deal once they get big. A fund promising twice the daily move has to reset itself every day. Suppose it holds $100 of investors' money and $200 of stock. If the stock falls 10%, the stock is worth $180 and the investors' money has fallen to $80. To be at 2x again, it can only hold $160 of stock, so it has to sell $20. If the stock rises 10%, it has to buy $20 derived. The fund buys after every rise and sells after every fall, in proportion to how big the move was.

Scale that up. On a 10% day, a $13 billion fund would have to sell or buy about $2.6 billion of stock, close to the end of the session derived. Goldman estimated that on a day when the market moves 5%, rebalancing tied to SK Hynix alone could exceed 20% of the stock's normal daily trading. None of that trading reflects a view about SK Hynix. It is arithmetic.

Put the two kinds of leverage together and you get a loop. Prices fall. Brokers sell the shares of borrowers who can no longer cover their loans, and funds sell to reset. That selling pushes prices lower, which brings on more of the same. It also runs in reverse, which explains the strangest feature of this year: the biggest falls and the biggest rises sit right next to each other. The KOSPI has risen 5% or more on 17 days this year, after six in the previous six years combined derived, and its best day ever came three days after one of its worst.

Leverage was already building before the Korean funds launched. Margin debt reached a 20-year high in February, and volatility from January to late May was already about 53%. But it rose to about 66% afterwards, and 17 of the year's 23 big falls have come since May 27 derived. The cost to the people holding these products has been severe. Korea's 2x SK Hynix fund had fallen more than 80% from its peak by late July, and the finance minister apologised in parliament. Because these funds reset daily, they can lose far more than twice the stock's fall: in one stretch SK Hynix fell 17.9% and a 2x product lost 47.5%.

Why the pause does not hold

Korea's circuit breaker stops trading for 20 minutes when the market is down 8%, so that people can think instead of panic. This year the index has touched that level on 12 separate days derived. On those days it recovered a little, from an average low of −9.7% to an average close of −7.9%, but on half of them it closed within a point of its low.

This fits what is doing the selling. A pause can calm a person. It cannot calm a margin call or a fund's daily reset, which are still waiting when trading starts again. What the record shows instead is a bounce the following day: the market rose seven times out of eleven, by about 2% on average derived. That is the pattern you would expect when forced sellers have pushed prices further than the news justified.

Prices and profits have come apart

The clearest sign that these moves are about ownership is what has been happening at the companies. In the three months to September, Samsung made ₩107.4 trillion of operating profit, the first time it has passed ₩100 trillion in a quarter. SK Hynix made ₩83.3 trillion, keeping 80 won of every 100 in sales as operating profit. Both were records. Yet SK Hynix's shares are 61% below their June peak and Samsung's 48% below theirs, and each company is now valued at only about two and a half times its operating profit, if the latest quarter is multiplied by four derived.

A multiple that low is not simply a bargain. Memory chips run in cycles, and investors plainly doubt that profits at this level can last. The product matters here too. Flash memory, the subject of an earlier note on SanDisk, is expected to loosen in the second half of 2027. DRAM, the fast memory where these two companies earn most of their money, is expected to stay tight, with TrendForce forecasting a wider gap between supply and demand next year. Doubts about the cycle may be justified one day. So far, though, the fall in these shares has run far ahead of anything in their results.

What happens next

The amplifiers are smaller than they were in June, but they are still there. Margin debt had come down to ₩30.2 trillion by late October. That is 22% below the peak, but still 10% above where the year began, even though the market outside the two chip stocks is lower than it was in January. Regulators have started to lean on it: since October 19, brokers can have no more than 15% of their margin loans in any single stock. The leveraged funds have shrunk, partly because their investors have lost so much, and the two companies' share of the market has fallen from 57% to 46%.

So the forces that made this year so violent are weakening slowly, and they have not gone. Until more of the borrowed money has left and more of these shares sit with owners who can wait, outside shocks will keep being turned into bigger moves than the news deserves, in both directions. Friday's fall was the latest example. The news came from New York. The crash was made in Korea.
