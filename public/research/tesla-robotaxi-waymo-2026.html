Research Note · No. 9

One Percent of Waymo

Tesla's latest results made its Robotaxi sound like a business that has arrived: driverless Cybercabs on the road in Austin, service in six cities, miles growing eightfold in a quarter. Put it next to Waymo, the company that has been doing this for years, and it looks very different.

Zhiye's Notes · October 24, 2026 · Figures from Tesla and Waymo through October 18, 2026

Verdict

False. Tesla's Robotaxi is real, but it is not yet a business. It has driven about 1% of Waymo's driverless miles, does not say how many rides it sells, and by its own account still costs too much to run.

With nobody in the driver's seat, Tesla's robotaxis have covered 3.1 million miles. Waymo's have covered 271.3 million. Tesla's total is about 1.1% of Waymo's derived — and Waymo's figure is three and a half months older.

Even in Austin, the one city where Tesla has been driving the longest, Waymo has gone about ten times as far. By March, Waymo was selling more than 500,000 paid rides a week. Tesla does not publish a ride count at all, and its finance chief said on the earnings call that the service has not yet reached its own cost target.

1. The claim, written so it can be wrong

Tesla started offering robotaxi rides in Austin in June 2025, with a Tesla employee sitting in the car. In 2026 it took the employees out, and on September 3 it started carrying riders in the Cybercab, a two-seater with no steering wheel or pedals. Its October results letter said robotaxi miles had grown eightfold in three months, and the Robotaxi app now lists six cities.

The claim. Tesla's Robotaxi has become a real business. It is no longer an experiment — it is a working taxi service on the same footing as the leaders.

The leader is Waymo, the self-driving company owned by Google's parent Alphabet. It has run driverless taxis for years and was operating in ten US cities as of March. That makes it the obvious measuring stick. The claim breaks into three predictions, each of which can be checked:

  • Prediction 1: Tesla's robotaxi operation is on a scale comparable with Waymo's.
  • Prediction 2: Tesla is selling rides in large numbers, and shows it.
  • Prediction 3: Tesla's robotaxis cost little enough to run to make money.

2. How big is it? About 1% of Waymo

The fairest single measure is miles driven with nobody at the wheel. Both companies now publish this on their own safety websites, and both count the same thing: miles with no human driver and no safety employee in the car.

Tesla's bar in the top panel is so short it is hard to see. Its 3.1 million miles are about 1.1% of Waymo's 271.3 million derived. Another way to put it: between April and June 2026, Waymo's total rose from 220.6 million to 271.3 million miles, about 557,000 miles a day derived. At that pace, Waymo drives Tesla's entire driverless total in under six days.

The bottom panel is the fairest possible match. Austin is where Tesla started and where 68% of its driverless miles have been driven derived. Waymo had already driven 21.1 million miles there by June. Tesla has driven 2.1 million — about one-tenth derived. Prediction 1 is false.

Waymo announced in March that it was giving more than 500,000 paid rides every week, up from about 50,000 a week in May 2024. It is aiming for a million a week by the end of 2026. Tesla has never published a weekly ride count, a fleet size or any revenue for the service — in its accounts, robotaxi money is mixed in with servicing, insurance and other items. The nearest thing on the earnings call was Elon Musk saying the Cybercab fleet in Austin had grown "by a factor of 10" in about a month, without saying from how many cars.

The city count looks closer, six against ten, but look at which cities. Five of Tesla's six — Austin, Dallas, Houston, Miami and Orlando — were already on Waymo's list in March. Tesla is mostly entering cities Waymo is already in, not opening ones Waymo has missed. Nothing Tesla has published shows rides being sold in large numbers. Prediction 2 is false.

4. Does it cost little enough? Not yet, Tesla says

A taxi only becomes a business when each mile costs less than riders pay for it. Tesla has set itself a target of 30 cents of cost for every paid mile. On the October earnings call, the company's finance chief, Vaibhav Taneja, was asked directly. His answer: "Are we at our goal of $0.30 today? No." He added that Tesla sees a path to get there by running the fleet at a larger scale. Prediction 3 is false — and that comes from Tesla itself.

5. How fast is it catching up?

Tesla is growing quickly. Its letter put driverless miles at nearly 2 million at the end of September. Its safety website put them at 3.1 million on October 18 — more than a million miles in 18 days, or roughly 60,000 a day derived.

That is a big jump, and it is still about a ninth of the 557,000 miles a day Waymo was driving back in April to June derived. Waymo is not standing still either: its weekly rides grew about tenfold in under two years. Tesla is speeding up from a very small base, while the company it is chasing keeps pulling further ahead in absolute terms every single day.

6. What the three answers add up to

None of the three predictions held. Tesla's robotaxi has driven about 1% of Waymo's driverless miles and about a tenth of Waymo's miles even in Austin. It publishes no ride count, no fleet size and no revenue. And its own finance chief says it does not yet run cheaply enough.

None of that means the service is fake. The cars really are driving people around six cities with nobody behind the wheel, and the numbers really are climbing fast. But a real business has customers it can count and costs that work. Today, Tesla's Robotaxi is a large and fast-growing test. Waymo's is the business.

Waymo's daily pace is the rise from 220.6 million miles at the end of March to 271.3 million at the end of June, over 91 days. Tesla's recent pace is the rise from "nearly two million" at the end of September to 3.1 million on October 18.
