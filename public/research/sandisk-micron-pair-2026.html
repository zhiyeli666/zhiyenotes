Research Note · No. 6

Why the Gap Never Closed

SanDisk and Micron both make memory chips, and both have soared on the AI boom. So a popular idea is to treat them as a pair: when SanDisk runs too far ahead, bet against it and buy Micron, and wait for the gap to close. The gap never closed. It tripled.

Zhiye's Notes · September 25, 2026 · Prices through September 23

Verdict

False. The two stocks move in the same direction, but they do not come back to the same place — and betting that they would lost money every way it was tested.

On most days they really do move together: same direction on 289 of 397 trading days. But $100 put into each on SanDisk's first day of trading is now $3,738 in SanDisk and $1,128 in Micron derived. SanDisk has been ahead ever since September 2025.

I tested the "bet on the gap closing" trade in 12 different versions. All 12 lost money — at least $286 each, betting $100 on each side.

The reason is simple. The gap is not a mistake waiting to be fixed. SanDisk's profits grew much faster than Micron's, and today the market charges almost exactly the same for a dollar of profit from either one: about $11.60 for SanDisk and $10.70 for Micron derived.

1. The claim, written so it can be wrong

A normal stock bet is about direction: you buy because you think the price will go up. A pair trade is a bet on the gap between two stocks instead. You buy the one that looks too cheap and "short" the one that looks too expensive. Shorting means betting a stock will fall: you borrow shares, sell them, and buy them back later. If the price goes up instead, you lose — and there is no limit to how much.

The appeal is that you do not have to guess where the market is heading. If both stocks fall, your short makes money and your purchase loses it. If both rise, the opposite. All that matters is whether the gap closes.

The claim. SanDisk and Micron are both AI memory stocks and they move together. When SanDisk runs too far ahead of Micron, bet against SanDisk and buy the same dollar amount of Micron. The two will drift back together and the bet will pay, whatever the market does.

It is easy to see why people believe it. Both companies make memory chips. Both are selling into the same AI data centres. Both just reported the best quarters in their history. Micron even makes the same kind of chip SanDisk does. To test the claim, I broke it into three predictions, each of which can be checked:

  • Prediction 1: the two stocks move together from day to day.
  • Prediction 2: when SanDisk gets ahead, the gap closes again.
  • Prediction 3: so betting on the gap closing makes money.

2. Do they move together? Yes

From SanDisk's first day of trading on its own, February 24, 2025, to September 23, 2026, there were 397 trading days. On 289 of them the two stocks moved in the same direction — both up on 167 days, both down on 122. That is 73% of the time derived.

Another way to measure this is a number called correlation, which runs from 0 (no link at all) to 1 (perfectly in step). For the two stocks' daily moves it is 0.75 derived. That is a strong link. Prediction 1 is true.

But there is a detail that turns out to matter a lot. They move the same way, not the same amount. On a typical day SanDisk moved 4.8% and Micron moved 3.5% derived. On the 289 days they went the same way, SanDisk went further on 199 of them.

3. Do they end up in the same place? No

Think of two cars on the same road. They turn at the same corners and stop at the same lights. But if one always drives 40% faster, they can point the same way for the whole trip and still end up miles apart.

That is what happened here. Figure 1 shows what $100 put into each stock on February 24, 2025 would be worth now.

SanDisk multiplied about 37 times. Micron multiplied about 11 times — an amazing result in any normal year, but less than a third of SanDisk's.

To see the gap on its own, divide SanDisk's $100 by Micron's $100. When the answer is 1, both have grown by the same amount. When it is 2, SanDisk has grown twice as much. Figure 2 tracks that number.

The gap sank to about 0.68 in June 2025, when SanDisk was the one lagging. Then it climbed to 4.4 by April 2026. It has wobbled back since — it dropped to about 2.9 in July — but every wobble started from a higher floor. It has not been back at 1 since September 4, 2025. Prediction 2 is false.

4. Testing the bet

A gap that keeps rising could still suit a pair trader, if it swung up and down enough along the way. So I tested the trade directly, using the rule pair traders usually start with:

The rule. Each day, compare the gap with its average over the last 60 trading days (about three months). If the gap is much higher than that average — further than it normally strays — bet $100 against SanDisk and put $100 into Micron. Close both bets when the gap falls back to its three-month average.

In 19 months the rule fired twice. Both bets lost.

Look at what happened to bet 1. Micron went up 153%, so the $100 in Micron did very well. But SanDisk went up 422%, so the bet against SanDisk lost $422. On its worst day the whole position was down $395 — nearly twice the $200 that was put on the table. Most brokers would have forced the trader to close it, or put in more money, long before that.

Notice also that the rule said "close the bet" on December 29 — not because the gap had closed, but because the three-month average had climbed up to meet it. The idea of "normal" kept moving. By the time the gap was back at "normal", normal was twice as high.

Maybe the rule was just set badly? So I ran it 12 ways: looking back over 20, 60 or 120 days; calling the gap "too wide" at two different distances; and either only betting against SanDisk, as the claim says, or also taking the opposite bet whenever SanDisk fell unusually far behind.

This is the trap in betting on a gap: most of the time it wobbles back a little and you collect small wins, until the one time it does not come back at all.

Prediction 3 is false.

5. Why the rubber band snapped

A pair trade needs a rubber band — something that pulls the two prices back together when they drift apart. For SanDisk and Micron, there is not one. They make different things, and their profits grew at very different speeds.

They sell different chips. SanDisk sells only NAND flash, the kind of memory that stores files and keeps them when the power is off — the chips in SSDs, phones and memory cards. Micron makes some NAND too, but three quarters of its sales are DRAM: fast working memory that forgets everything when the power goes off, including the HBM stacked next to AI chips. The two kinds of chip are sold in separate markets, and their prices can move at very different speeds.

Here is the surprising part. Sales grew by almost the same amount at both — about four and a half times. Yet SanDisk's profit per share grew 135 times and Micron's grew 13 times. How can that be?

It comes down to where each one started. Imagine two lemonade stands that both sell cups for $1. The first spends 74¢ making each cup and keeps 26¢. The second spends 62¢ and keeps 38¢. Now lemonade prices shoot up, and both stands end up keeping 85¢ of every dollar. The first stand's profit per cup has more than tripled. The second's has a bit more than doubled. Add in that both are now selling far more cups, and the stand that started with the thinner profit comes out much further ahead.

That is what drove SanDisk's lead. Its sales are all NAND, NAND prices rose fast — SanDisk said two thirds of its sales jump in the latest quarter came from higher prices — and it started with the thinnest profit. So its profits grew far faster than Micron's.

And the share prices simply followed the profits. Over the past year SanDisk's price rose 17 times and Micron's 6.5 times, a smaller gap than the gap in profits. The cleanest check is to ask how much the market charges for each dollar of profit. Take each company's latest quarterly profit per share, multiply by four for a full year, and divide the share price by it:

6. What the two answers add up to

Pair trades work when there is something that forces two prices back together. The textbook example is Alphabet's two kinds of shares, GOOGL and GOOG. They are slices of the same company with the same profits, so if one gets ahead of the other, the gap has to close. That is a real rubber band.

SanDisk and Micron do not have one. They are two different companies selling two different kinds of memory, and the prices of those chips move on their own. Moving in the same direction on 73% of days made them look tied together. But what decides where a stock ends up is the profit behind it, and SanDisk's profit ran away from Micron's.

So the claim fails at the point that matters. "They move together" is true. "So the gap will close" is false, and the trade built on it lost money in all 12 versions tested. When SanDisk ran ahead of Micron, it was not a mistake for the market to correct. It was the market keeping up with the profits.

How the test works. Prices are daily closes from February 24, 2025 (SanDisk's first day of regular trading after leaving Western Digital) to September 23, 2026, adjusted for Micron's dividends. The "gap" in the trading test is the ratio of the two share prices. "Much higher than its average" means the gap was more than 1.5 or 2 standard deviations above its rolling average over the look-back period, measured on a log scale. Each bet puts equal dollars on the two sides and is closed when the gap returns to its rolling average; every bet in Table 2 had closed by September 23. Borrowing costs and trading fees are left out, so real results would have been a little lower still. "Price for each $1 of yearly profit" is a simple price-to-earnings ratio on the latest quarter's adjusted profit per share times four. Micron reports its next quarter on September 30, 2026, after this note was written.
