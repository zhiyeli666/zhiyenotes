Why the Gap Came Back

SanDisk and Micron both make memory chips, and both have soared on the AI boom. A popular way to trade them is as a pair: when SanDisk runs too far ahead, bet against it and buy Micron, and wait for the gap to close. Over the last six months, the gap kept closing.

Zhiye's Notes · September 26, 2026 · Prices from April 1 to September 23

Verdict

True. Since April the two stocks have moved together, the gap between them kept coming back, and betting on it closing made money in all 12 versions tested.

They moved in the same direction on 97 of 121 trading days. $100 put into each at the end of March is now $286 in SanDisk and $317 in Micron derived — close together, after the lead changed hands 13 times.

The standard version of the trade placed one bet, and it made $22 on $100 a side in five weeks — during which both stocks went up by more than 60%. All 12 versions tested came out ahead, by $446 in total derived.

The reason is what held the two together: profit. At the end of March, SanDisk cost $25.60 for each $1 of yearly profit and Micron cost $6.90. Today it is $11.60 and $10.70 derived. When one stock's price runs far ahead of its profits, the gap does not last.

1. The claim, written so it can be wrong

A normal stock bet is about direction: you buy because you think the price will go up. A pair trade is a bet on the gap between two stocks instead. You buy the one that looks too cheap and "short" the one that looks too expensive. Shorting means betting a stock will fall: you borrow shares, sell them, and buy them back later. If the price goes up instead, you lose.

The appeal is that you do not have to guess where the market is heading. If both stocks fall, your short makes money and your purchase loses it. If both rise, the opposite. All that matters is whether the gap closes.

The claim. SanDisk and Micron are both AI memory stocks and they move together. When SanDisk runs too far ahead of Micron, bet against SanDisk and buy the same dollar amount of Micron. The two will drift back together and the bet will pay, whatever the market does.

This note tests it over the six months from April 1 to September 23, 2026, broken into three predictions that can each be checked:

  • Prediction 1: the two stocks move together from day to day.
  • Prediction 2: when one gets ahead, the gap closes again.
  • Prediction 3: so betting on the gap closing makes money.

2. Do they move together? Yes

There were 121 trading days from April 1 to September 23. On 97 of them the two stocks moved in the same direction — both up on 54 days, both down on 43. That is 80% of the time derived.

Another way to measure this is a number called correlation, which runs from 0 (no link at all) to 1 (perfectly in step). For the two stocks' daily moves it is 0.83 derived. That is a very strong link.

SanDisk is the jumpier of the two. On a typical day it moved 5.7% and Micron moved 4.4% derived. But the bigger swings went both ways — up and down — so they kept cancelling out. Prediction 1 is true.

3. Does the gap come back? Yes

Figure 1 shows what $100 put into each stock at the close on March 31, 2026 would be worth each day since.

The two lines wrap around each other the whole way. Both more than tripled by late June, both fell back hard in July, and both climbed again after that. By September 23 they were only $31 apart.

To see the gap on its own, divide SanDisk's $100 by Micron's $100. When the answer is 1, both have grown by the same amount. Above 1, SanDisk is ahead; below 1, Micron is. Figure 2 tracks that number.

The gap never runs away. SanDisk got 19% ahead on April 13 and it came back. SanDisk fell 29% behind on July 28 and that came back too. The line crossed the 1× level 13 times in six months derived. Prediction 2 is true.

4. Testing the bet

A gap that keeps coming back is exactly what a pair trader wants. To check whether that was enough to make money, I tested the trade directly, using the rule pair traders usually start with:

The rule. Each day, compare the gap with its average over the last 60 trading days (about three months). If the gap is much higher than that average — further than it normally strays — bet $100 against SanDisk and put $100 into Micron. Close both bets when the gap falls back to its three-month average.

Between April and September the rule fired once, and the bet made money.

5. What held them together

A pair trade needs a rubber band — something that pulls the two prices back together when they drift apart. For SanDisk and Micron, that something is profit.

The fairest way to compare two share prices is to ask how much you pay for each dollar of profit. Take a company's latest quarterly profit per share, multiply by four for a full year, and divide the share price by it. Here is that sum with what investors could see on March 31, and again today:

On March 31, a dollar of SanDisk's profit cost almost four times as much as a dollar of Micron's. That was the stretched rubber band. SanDisk's price had raced ahead of what it was actually earning, because investors expected its profits to jump.

Then they did. SanDisk's profit per share went from $6.20 to $23.41 to $39.25 in two quarters — more than six times higher. Micron's roughly doubled, from $12.20 to $25.11. Meanwhile Micron's share price, which had been the cheap one, rose a little faster than SanDisk's. Both moves pulled in the same direction, and today the two cost almost exactly the same per dollar of profit.

The businesses themselves came together too. In the quarters that were public on March 31, SanDisk kept 51¢ of every $1 of sales after paying to make its chips, and Micron kept 74¢. In the latest quarters both keep exactly 84.6¢. Two companies earning the same way, on the same AI demand, ended up priced the same way.

6. What the two answers add up to

Every pair trade rests on one question: is there something that forces the two prices back together? The textbook example is Alphabet's two kinds of shares, GOOGL and GOOG — slices of the same company with the same profits, so a gap between them has to close.

SanDisk and Micron are two different companies, but over these six months they behaved like a pair with a real rubber band. They moved the same way on 80% of days. When SanDisk's price got ahead of its profits in April, its profits caught up and the gap shrank. When Micron pulled ahead in July, that gap shrank too. The lead changed hands 13 times.

So the claim holds on all three counts. They move together, the gap comes back, and the trade built on it made money in all 12 versions tested. The thing to watch is not the share prices but what each one costs per dollar of profit. In April that number was nearly four to one, and it told you which way the gap was going to move.

"Much higher than its average" means the gap was more than 1.5 or 2 standard deviations above its rolling average over the look-back period, measured on a log scale; where a look-back reaches before April, it uses the earlier prices, but every bet was placed between April 1 and September 23. Where the signal was already on at the start, the bet opens on April 1. Each bet puts equal dollars on the two sides and is closed when the gap returns to its rolling average. Borrowing costs and trading fees are left out. "Price for each $1 of yearly profit" is a simple price-to-earnings ratio on the latest reported quarter's adjusted profit per share times four.
