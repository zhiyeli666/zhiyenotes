Research Note · No. 5

A Car Company, Priced as Something Else

Everyone now says Tesla is an AI and robotics company, not a car maker. Its own accounts say otherwise: 73% of what it earns comes from selling cars. The odd part is that only about a third of what it costs does.

Zhiye's Notes · September 18, 2026 · Data through September 17

Verdict

False about the business, true about the stock. Tesla earns like a car company and is priced like something that does not exist yet.

In the last reported quarter, 73% of Tesla's revenue came from selling cars. Energy was 11% and services 16%. Robotaxi revenue was not broken out at all, and the first Optimus robots being built are for collecting training data, not for customers. As a business, the answer is not close.

As a stock it is the reverse. Outside estimates put Robotaxi at about 45% of Tesla's value and Optimus at about 19% — roughly two thirds of a $1.4 trillion company resting on two businesses with no disclosed sales.

And there is a clean way to show that nobody can actually measure that two thirds. Right now the lowest analyst target for Tesla is $24.86 and the highest is $505. That is a twenty-fold gap between professionals looking at the same company on the same day.

1. The claim, written so it can be wrong

"Tesla is not a car company any more" has become the standard thing to say about it. Sometimes it is said as praise and sometimes as a warning, but it is always said as though it were settled fact.

The claim. Tesla has become an artificial intelligence and robotics company. The car business is now the smaller, older part of what it does, and judging Tesla by car sales misses the point.

There are two completely different things this could mean, and mixing them up is where the confusion lives. It could mean Tesla's business is mostly not cars. Or it could mean Tesla's share price is mostly not about cars. Those have different answers, so this note measures both.

Nearly three quarters of the money still comes from putting cars on trucks and shipping them to people. The two businesses the story is about — robotaxis and humanoid robots — contribute so little that one is not reported separately and the other has not sold a unit. A few Cybercabs are carrying paying passengers in Austin, which is real but tiny.

So on the business question the claim is simply false, and it is not a close or arguable false. Tesla is a car company that is trying to become something else.

3. Where the price comes from

Now the same question asked of the share price, which is a different question with an opposite answer.

Tesla is worth about $1.4 trillion. Outside analysts who split that value into pieces put roughly 45% on Robotaxi and 19% on Optimus. Everything else — every car, every battery, every service centre, the whole 73% that actually earns the money — is the remaining third.

Two thirds of $1.4 trillion is about $900 billion derived, assigned to a taxi service that does not report revenue and a robot that has not been sold. That figure is larger than all but a handful of companies on earth are worth in total.

4. How we know nobody can measure it

An estimate like "45% Robotaxi" invites an obvious question: how confident is anyone in that? There is a neat way to answer it without arguing about anybody's model.

Twenty-five Wall Street analysts publish a target price for Tesla. The average is $388.85, close to today's $366. But averages hide everything here. The lowest target is $24.86. The highest is $505, and one firm carries $600.

The spread is the measurement. When analysts land within a narrow band, it means the business can be counted and they are arguing about small things. A twenty-fold gap means they are not really valuing a business at all — they are voting on whether a story will come true. One side is pricing a car maker with thin margins. The other is pricing the largest robotics company in history. Both are looking at the same accounts.

5. What the two answers add up to

Put the two halves together and the picture is clear and slightly uncomfortable.

Tesla is a car company. That is what its accounts say, at 73%, and it is not a marginal call. It is also a car company whose profits from cars are currently thin — the operating margin fell to 1.4% last quarter as it poured money into the other things, with capital spending above $25 billion this year, roughly three times its historic level.

And the share price is not really about any of that. It is a price for Robotaxi and Optimus working, attached to a car business that is paying the bills in the meantime. Which means the question that decides whether $366 is cheap or expensive is not "how many cars will Tesla sell". It is "will a robotaxi network and a humanoid robot become real businesses" — and the honest state of the evidence on that is a range from $24.86 to $505.
