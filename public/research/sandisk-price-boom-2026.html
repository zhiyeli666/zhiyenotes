Research Note · No. 10

A Price Boom, Not an AI Boom

SanDisk is traded as an AI stock, and its results read like one: data-center sales up more than fivefold in a year, 85 cents of every sales dollar kept after making the chips. Dig into its annual report and a different company appears. Most of the extra money came from selling ordinary chips — for phones, laptops and cars — at nearly three times the old price.

Zhiye's Notes · November 1, 2026 · Results for the quarter ended October 2, 2026 · Share prices before the November stock split

Verdict

False. SanDisk's boom is a price boom that AI set off, not an AI sales boom. That makes its profits a bet on flash prices — and the industry's own forecasters expect the shortage behind those prices to end in the second half of 2027.

In fiscal 2026, SanDisk sold only about 15% more storage than the year before, yet its revenue rose 175%. Almost two-thirds of the extra revenue came from its Edge business — chips for phones, PCs and cars — where volume barely grew and the price per gigabyte nearly tripled derived.

Because its costs hardly moved, almost all of its profit is price. A stress test shows SanDisk would still break even if flash prices fell about 80% — which is roughly where they were in 2025. And in the market that is actually tied to AI, drives for data centers, SanDisk is the smallest of the five big makers.

1. The claim, written so it can be wrong

SanDisk makes NAND flash, the memory that stores data even when the power is off. Over the past year it has become one of the hottest stocks in the market, and the explanation is always the same: artificial intelligence. AI data centers need enormous amounts of fast storage, and SanDisk sells it.

The claim. SanDisk's boom is an AI boom. It is making record profits because AI data centers are buying its chips in huge numbers, and that demand is here to stay.

To test it, this note asks four questions. Where did the extra money actually come from? Why did a small shortage produce such enormous profits? How much of those profits would survive if prices fell? And how strong is SanDisk in the part of the market that really is driven by AI?

Part one · Where the money came from

2. Price, not volume

Revenue can grow in only two ways: selling more, or charging more. Most companies never tell you which. SanDisk does, in one paragraph of its annual report, and that paragraph changes the whole story.

For the year to July 2026, SanDisk's revenue grew from $7.4 billion to $20.2 billion. But the amount of storage it sold, measured in exabytes, grew by only "mid-teens percent". Selling about 15% more while earning 175% more means the average price per gigabyte rose by roughly 140% — about two and a half times the year before derived.

Split by business, the pattern sharpens. The data-center business really did grow its volume — almost 120% more storage sold — and AI is the reason. But the Edge business, which sells flash for phones, laptops, cars and other devices, sold only a high-single-digit percentage more, and charged almost 180% more per gigabyte. In consumer products like memory cards, SanDisk sold less than the year before and still made more money.

Now weigh the businesses by how much they added. Of the $12.9 billion of extra revenue, Edge supplied $8.0 billion, data centers $4.2 billion and consumer $0.7 billion. That is 62% from Edge, and most of it was price derived.

The cost side makes the point even clearer. SanDisk's cost of making what it sold rose only 12% over the year, from $5.1 billion to $5.8 billion, while the amount sold rose about 15% — so the cost of each gigabyte barely changed derived. Same factories, same chips, same cost, far higher price. Nearly all of the jump in profit is that gap.

So why do phone chips cost so much more because of AI? Because they come out of the same factories. SanDisk gets all of its flash from fabs it shares with Kioxia in Japan, and every maker in the industry faces the same choice of where to send each wafer. When AI data centers started buying every high-capacity drive they could get, they pulled supply away from everyone else, and the price rose for every kind of flash at once. AI did not make SanDisk sell many more phone chips. It made every phone chip more expensive.

In the latest quarter the mix shifted: SanDisk said about 60% of its growth over the previous quarter came from selling more and about 40% from higher prices, and data-center sales rose 61% in three months. But a quarter of extra volume does not change what the annual numbers show about where its profit level comes from. SanDisk keeps 85 cents of every sales dollar because flash costs several times what it costs to make — and that is a statement about price.

Part two · Why prices went so high

3. How a 4% shortage makes 85% margins

Here is the strangest number in this story. TrendForce, the research firm that tracks the memory industry most closely, estimates that the whole NAND flash market will end 2026 just 4–5% short of what buyers want. A gap that small helped push SanDisk's average price per gigabyte to about two and a half times the year before.

That is how commodities with no substitutes behave. A cloud company building an AI data center cannot swap flash for something else, and it cannot leave the servers empty while it waits. When there is not quite enough to go round, the buyers who need it most simply bid higher until someone else drops out. The price does not rise by 5% to close a 5% gap. It rises until enough people give up.

And people are giving up. TrendForce expects global smartphone production to fall 15–20% in 2026 and laptop shipments about 10%, both because memory has made devices more expensive. SanDisk's own consumer business sold mid-teens percent fewer exabytes. High prices are destroying demand at the cheap end of the market, and every chip a phone maker does not buy is a chip freed up for someone else.

The same mechanism works in reverse, and it is just as violent. If a 4–5% shortage can more than double the price, a small surplus can crash it. A memory boom does not need demand to collapse in order to end. It only needs supply to edge slightly ahead.

TrendForce expects exactly that to begin in 2027. Its July forecast says suppliers will keep adding output by moving to chips with more layers, that Chinese makers will raise their share of world flash output to nearly 19% as new equipment comes online, and that phones and laptops will stay weak. Its conclusion: the market's balance "is expected to turn positive in the second half of 2027." In plain words, the shortage ends.

Part three · The stress test

4. What happens to profit when prices fall

If SanDisk's profit is mostly price, the obvious question is how much survives a fall in price. Its latest quarter makes this easy to work out, because its costs are so small.

In the quarter to October 2, SanDisk had $11.0 billion of sales. Making the chips cost about $1.6 billion, and running the company — research, sales, staff — cost about $0.5 billion. Everything else, about $8.9 billion, was operating profit derived. Now hold the volume and costs fixed and lower only the price.

The line shows two things. First, profit falls much faster than price. A 25% fall in price cuts profit by about 31%; a 50% fall cuts it by about 62% derived. That is because the price cut comes straight out of profit while the costs stay put.

Second, SanDisk does not lose money until prices fall about 80% derived. That sounds like an enormous cushion — until you ask where prices were before the boom. In fiscal 2025 SanDisk kept 30 cents of each sales dollar after making its chips; now it keeps 85. With costs roughly unchanged, that means today's prices are about four and a half times the 2025 level, and a return to 2025 prices would be a fall of about 78% derived. In other words, the whole of SanDisk's current profit is the distance between today's flash price and the price of a year and a half ago.

The share price already expects some of that distance to close. At $1,330 before the results, SanDisk was valued as if its long-run yearly profit would be about 70% below today's pace, if it were priced like an average large US company. In this test, that is roughly what a 55% price fall would do derived. Investors are betting on a big fall in flash prices — but not on a full return to 2025.

5. How much the contracts protect

SanDisk's answer to this risk is its new long-term supply contracts. About half of what it expects to sell in fiscal 2027, and about two-thirds of fiscal 2028, is now covered by multi-year deals, backed — for the first five of them — by more than $11 billion of financial guarantees.

"There are now very substantial customers that do not want to play the quarter-by-quarter price game."

— David Goeckeler, SanDisk CEO, April 2026 earnings call

Run the stress test through the contracts. If half of fiscal 2027's sales are at fixed prices and the other half fall 50%, the average price falls only 25%, and profit drops by about 31% instead of 62% derived. For the next year or so, that is real protection.

It fades with time, though. SanDisk's finance chief explained in April that within each contract, the near-term part is mostly fixed and "the longer out you go, there is more variable." In 2028, after TrendForce expects supply to have caught up, much of the contracted volume will be priced closer to whatever the market says. The contracts guarantee that customers keep buying. They guarantee much less about what those customers will pay.

Part four · The competition

6. Smallest where AI matters most

If the price boom fades, what will matter is who sells the most flash that AI actually needs: big, fast drives for data centers, called enterprise SSDs. This is where SanDisk is weakest.

Samsung alone sold almost five times as much data-center storage as SanDisk. SK hynix, with its Solidigm business, sold nearly three times as much. Even Kioxia, which shares SanDisk's factories, sold more than half again as much. SanDisk's data-center business is growing fast — its high-capacity QLC drives have just entered volume production — but it is growing from the back of the pack.

SanDisk has also been choosing price over size. In the April–June quarter, its revenue grew the least of the five big flash makers, about 51% against 70–99% for the others, which TrendForce put down to SanDisk being "conservative" about how much it shipped. While the shortage lasts, holding back supply keeps prices high and margins fat: in the spring quarter SanDisk kept 84.6 cents of each sales dollar after making its chips, a little above Kioxia's roughly 80 cents on wafers from the same fabs. But a strategy of selling less to earn more per chip works only as long as buyers have nowhere else to go.

And its factories are not entirely its own. SanDisk owns 49.9% of the Flash Ventures fabs and takes roughly half their output; Kioxia takes the other half and sells it in the same markets. That is also why SanDisk's spending on factories looks so small — about $177 million of its own equipment purchases last year — because most of the cost of building fabs sits inside the joint venture. Low spending on SanDisk's books is a feature of how it is organised, not proof that the industry has stopped building.

7. What it adds up to

The claim fails on its own terms. SanDisk's record profits are not mainly the result of AI data centers buying its chips. AI set off an industry-wide shortage, and SanDisk's profit came from the price that shortage created — above all on the ordinary chips for phones, laptops and cars that make up most of its business.

That matters because price is the most fragile part of a memory company's earnings. SanDisk's costs barely moved, so nearly all its profit is the gap between today's price and the price of 2025. The shortage behind that price is only a few percent of supply, high prices are already shrinking demand from phones and laptops, and TrendForce expects supply to overtake demand in the second half of 2027. SanDisk's contracts will soften the first part of any fall, but they lock in volume more firmly than price — and in the AI drives that will keep growing after the shortage ends, SanDisk is the smallest of the five.

SanDisk may well stay a very profitable company. But it is a flash-price company that AI made rich, not an AI company that happens to make flash.
